TITLE: Prenatal Detection of Fetal Abdominal Cysts: Can We Reassure Future Parents?

=== ABSTRACT ===
This study aimed to evaluate the perinatal outcome of fetal abdominal cysts based on the timing of prenatal diagnosis and identify prenatal characteristics associated with postnatal surgical intervention.
Fetuses with prenatally detected isolated abdominal cysts referred between January 2007 and December 2022 were included. Fetuses with multiple congenital anomalies or cysts suspected of renal or spinal origin were excluded.
Among the 253 included cases, 67.6% (171/253) of the abdominal cysts regressed spontaneously either prenatally or postnatally. Persistent cysts were observed during postnatal follow‐up in 28.9% (73/253) and in 3.6% (9/253) the suspected abdominal cyst was reclassified as another anomaly during postnatal follow‐up. Two cases of a transient cyst diagnosed in the first trimester required surgical intervention postpartum. Surgical intervention was performed in 16.2%. Multivariate analysis showed that prenatal cysts > 40 mm were strongly associated with surgical intervention (OR 10.25, 95% CI 4.08–25.74). Diagnosis before 16 weeks (OR 5.03, 95% CI 1.04–24.42) and between 16 and 24 weeks (OR 6.42, 95% CI 2.49–16.51) was also linked to higher odds of surgery compared with diagnosis after 24 weeks, whereas gender showed no significant association.
Isolated fetal abdominal cysts have a good prognosis with a high rate of spontaneous regression (67.6%) and a low rate of surgical intervention (16.2%). However, especially first trimester cysts carry a significant risk of persistent lesions necessitating surgical intervention for which follow‐up is recommended, even when prenatal regression is suspected.

## INTRODUCTION
The widespread use of prenatal sonography along with the simultaneous improvement in the quality of ultrasound has led to an increase in the prenatal detection of fetal abdominal cysts. [1, 2] When excluding cystic masses originating from the renal tract, the most prevalent etiology of fetal abdominal cysts is ovarian cysts, enteric duplication cysts, hepatic and biliary cysts, meconium pseudocysts and mesenteric cysts. [3] The most common sonographic presentation, regardless of the underlying etiology, is an oval, anechoic structure of variable size and position. [3] Of the abdominal cysts, ovarian cysts are the most prevalent and are typically located in the lower abdomen in female fetuses. However, accurate prenatal diagnosis of the specific origin of the cyst can be challenging due to the resemblance between abdominal cysts of different etiologies.
In Dutch clinical practice, abdominal cysts are most frequently observed in the second trimester, as all women are offered a second trimester anomaly scan, but third trimester ultrasonography is not part of standard obstetric care in the Netherlands. [4] However, cysts of ovarian and gastrointestinal origin are predominantly found on scans in the third trimester due to hormonal changes and increased gastric motility. [3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10].
In previous literature, the overall prognosis of fetal abdominal cysts has been described as favorable and isolated abdominal cysts are generally not associated with chromosomal anomalies. [11, 12] Fewer cases of first trimester abdominal cysts have been reported but have been found to be associated with a higher risk of adverse outcome or associated anomalies such as anorectal malformations, even if the cyst regresses prenatally. [13, 14] In contrast, ovarian cysts are more often described as benign and are most often diagnosed in the third trimester. [3, 10] Therefore, timing of the prenatal detection of the cyst might be associated with perinatal outcome.
In addition to differences in the natural course of fetal abdominal cysts, there is a great heterogeneity in perinatal monitoring and treatment. For example, in some countries, the prenatal aspiration of bigger cysts has been described to preserve the ovary in case of ovarian cysts, whilst other studies report on beneficial effects of conservative management. [10, 15] Regarding postnatal management, previous studies reported a favorable neonatal outcome after both postnatal surgical and conservative management of abdominal cysts. [5, 16, 17] Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the perinatal outcomes of fetuses with abdominal cysts in a Dutch cohort in relation to the timing of prenatal and to identify prenatal characteristics associated with postnatal surgical intervention.

## MATERIAL AND METHODS
This study retrospectively reviewed cases that underwent advanced ultrasound examination (AUE) for suspected fetal abdominal cysts and were referred to the Amsterdam University Medical Center (AUMC) and the Groningen University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG) between January 2007 and December 2022. Only cases of isolated fetal abdominal cysts based on ultrasound findings were included. Fetuses presenting with multiple congenital anomalies on ultrasound, cases with confirmed associated chromosomal or genetic fetal anomalies identified through chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis and cysts with a prenatally renal or spinal origin were excluded. In addition, cases with unknown pregnancy outcomes or women who did not consent to the use of their data for research were excluded. This study received approval from the Medical Ethics Review Committee (reference number: W21_361 # 21.401).
The primary outcome in this study was the perinatal outcome of fetal abdominal cysts. To assess the influence of the timing (gestational age and trimester of pregnancy) of prenatal diagnosis on perinatal outcomes, cases were stratified into three groups: cases detected before 16 weeks of gestation (thereby including cases referred after the first trimester anomaly scan), cases detected between 16 and 24 weeks of gestation (including cases referred after the second trimester anomaly scan) and cases referred after 24 weeks of gestation. Pre‐ and postnatal characteristics were analyzed between conservative management and surgical treatment to identify factors associated with surgical intervention.
Since the introduction of the second trimester anomaly scan in the Dutch national screening program in 2007, this scan is offered at around 20 weeks of gestation in all pregnancies. In addition, from September 2021 onwards, the first trimester anomaly scan is offered in research settings between 12 + 4 – 14 + 3 weeks of gestation [18]. Third trimester ultrasound examination is not offered as part of standard obstetric care and is performed only for obstetric indications such as assessment of fetal growth and amniotic fluid assessment or localization of the placenta. If a structural anomaly such as a fetal abdominal cyst is suspected during ultrasound examination, patients are referred to a fetal medicine unit for advanced ultrasound examination, evaluation in a multidisciplinary team and a hospital delivery is advised when indicated. The AUMC and UMCG are tertiary centers for fetal medicine, pediatric and adolescent gynecology and pediatric surgery.
Prenatal data were collected through the digitalized ultrasound database, ASTRAIA Software GmbH, Copyright 2000–2025, version 29.1.1. The primary search contained cases in which the selection criteria ‘abdominal cyst’ was selected. In addition, a second search encompassed all cases in which the final conclusion included the word ‘cyst’. All reports were assessed for study eligibility. Postnatal data were extracted from EPIC, electronic medical record software. Collected data included maternal and ultrasound characteristics, perinatal and postnatal outcomes. To differentiate between small and large cysts, the cutoff size of 40 mm proposed by previous studies was applied [10, 12, 19, 20]. Prenatal regression was defined as cases where the cyst could no longer be visualized during follow‐up ultrasound examination. After birth, neonates were monitored primarily by ultrasound until either regression of the cyst had been reported, in case of gradual decrease of the cyst (and therefore was decided to end the follow‐up), or if a surgical intervention had taken place. Pre‐ and postnatal diagnoses were compared to assess the concordance regarding the origin of the cyst.
Data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28.0). The numerical variables were described by frequency, mean and standard deviation (SD) if distributed normally, or by median and range in case of a skewed distribution. Numerical variables were compared using a Student‐t test (with normal distribution) or Mann‐Whitney U test (with skewed distribution). p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To analyze the association between prenatal characteristics (timing of diagnosis (< 16 weeks, 16–24 weeks, > 24 weeks), maximum prenatal cyst size < 40 mm or > 40 mm, gender) and the need of surgical intervention, a multivariate logistic regression was performed. Preliminary analyses were conducted to assess confounding, effect modification and multicollinearity. Additionally, the adjusted R‐squared was reported to show the proportion of the variance in the outcomes explained by the model. p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## RESULTS
Between January 2007 and December 2022, a total of 274 cases with fetal abdominal cysts were referred. Of these, 21 cases were excluded from analysis due to loss of follow‐up (n = 11) or lack of consent for data use in research (n = 10). Hence, 253 cases were included in the analysis. Diagnosis before 16 weeks of gestation occurred in 5.5% (14/253), 33.2% (84/253) were diagnosed between 16 and 24 weeks of gestation and 61.3% (155/253) were diagnosed after 24 weeks of gestation (Table 1).

In another case, an anorectal malformation was diagnosed after a suspected duplication cyst diagnosed in the first trimester could no longer be visualized from 16 weeks onwards. Of the remaining cases, postnatal ultrasound was performed in 18 cases to confirm regression of the cyst, in which two cases with suspected regression were classified as a renal and adrenal cyst postpartum. ## DISCUSSION
This study demonstrates a high rate of spontaneous regression in cases with isolated fetal abdominal cysts (171/253, 67.6%), of which the majority did not receive surgical treatment (212/253, 83.8%). After birth, the suspected abdominal cyst was reclassified as another anomaly during follow‐up in 3.6% (9/253). Of the cases with suspected prenatal regression, two cases of a transient cyst diagnosed in the first trimester required surgical intervention postpartum. In contrast, we found that cysts diagnosed after 24 weeks are less frequently associated with the need for surgery.
This multicenter study evaluated outcomes in a large cohort of isolated fetal abdominal cysts within the Dutch national screening program, where routine third‐trimester ultrasound screening is not routinely performed. Restricting the analysis to isolated fetal abdominal cysts eliminates the possible influence of associated anomalies on prognosis, but therefore provides restricted value for prognostic counseling of non‐isolated cysts. By including cysts diagnosed from the first trimester onwards, we found that cysts diagnosed before 16 weeks of gestation, even if transient, warrant careful postpartum follow‐up. In 38.7% (98/253) of the cases the abdominal cyst was diagnosed before 24 weeks of gestation within the timeframe or prior the second trimester anomaly scan. However, the majority of the fetuses (155/253, 61.3%) with abdominal cysts were detected after 24 weeks of gestation in the (late) second or third trimester. Therefore, the true prevalence of abdominal cysts occurring in the third trimester might be underestimated in the Dutch population, since routine ultrasound examination in the third trimester is not part of standard obstetric care. This may introduce bias, reflected in higher surgical rates for cysts diagnosed in the third trimester, because larger, clinically significant cysts are more likely to be detected. Previous studies have reported an increase in the uptake of abdominal cysts when performing routine ultrasound scanning in the third trimester. [8, 21] Furthermore, because of the retrospective design of this study, specific prenatal diagnoses were missing for a portion of the cases, precluding the assessment of concordance between pre‐ and postnatal diagnoses for these cysts.
Prenatal regression rates of this cohort of 28.3% are comparable to previous studies that reported spontaneous in utero regression of fetal abdominal cysts in 22%–29%. [22, 23] In this cohort, routine postnatal follow‐up ultrasound for cases with suspected prenatal regression of the cyst was not yet routinely agreed upon because of the low risk of a persistent symptomatic cyst. In 2/53 (3.7%) of the cases with suspected prenatal regression of the cyst, the neonate received surgical treatment postpartum, including one case of anorectal malformation and one torsion of a duplication cyst. Both of these cases were diagnosed with a transient cyst in the first trimester. However, case reports of anorectal malformations after a lower abdominal cyst in the first trimester have been described. [13, 24] Consequently, in prenatally regressing cysts diagnosed in the first trimester, the possibility of a persistent lesion requiring surgical management should be considered.
Of the persistent cysts postpartum, a specific prenatal diagnosis on the origin of the cyst was reported in 106/144. The concordance of the pre‐ and postnatal diagnosis was obtained in 82/106 (77.4%). The highest correspondence between pre‐ and postnatal diagnosis was reported in the persistent ovarian, splenic and hepatic cysts (correspondence 58.8%, 85.7% and 75% respectively). However, definitive conclusions on correspondence rates are more challenging in the cysts with low incidence within the cohort. Catania et al. reported a similar high correspondence rate in the group of simple ovarian cysts, along with high rates of correct diagnosis in the meconium pseudocyst subgroup [5], which were not diagnosed in our cohort. On the contrary, none of the enteric duplications in the current study (28/141 of the persistent cysts 19.9%) received this specific prenatal diagnosis. These cysts were most frequently labeled as non‐specified abdominal cysts, which is in line with findings of previous studies. [1, 5, 22, 25] A possible explanation is the difficulty of defining the typical ‘double wall aspect’ of enteric duplications on prenatal ultrasound. As this is not always (or only partially) identified, other differential diagnoses remain possible. [26] Previous studies have reported an increased prenatal specific diagnostic rate through additional MRI imaging, which was not a standard procedure within our study. [17, 27, 28] Future research should consider the possible prognostic gain of MRI, weighed against the additional costs and outcomes associated with extra imaging, even more so in a relatively benign anomaly such as the fetal abdominal cyst. Lastly, in 9/253 (3.6%), a different anomaly than the abdominal cyst was diagnosed postpartum, which has also been reported in previous studies. [1, 29, 30] Therefore, general evaluation by a pediatric surgeon or pediatric and adolescent gynecologist and simultaneous ultrasound examination in children with prenatally persistent cysts in the first weeks of life will aid in confirmation of the prenatal diagnosis and provide reassurance to parents. Because obtaining a definitive diagnosis prenatally can be challenging, prenatal counseling should address that an alternative diagnosis may arise postpartum as well as the inherent residual risk that certain anomalies may go unseen on prenatal ultrasound.
In total, 16.2% (41/253) of the prenatally identified cysts postnatal surgery was performed. This is relatively low compared to other studies in which postnatal surgery was performed in 29%–81%. [1, 5, 17, 23, 30, 31] Lv et al. included large numbers of choledochal cysts and sacrococcygeal teratomas which required surgery, [17] whereas this study only found choledochal cysts in 6/141 (4.2%) of the persistent cysts and one abdominal teratoma, which may have resulted in lower surgical rates within this cohort. Catania et al. reported the highest percentage of surgical interventions (81%), which was attributed to a relatively high percentage of gastro‐intestinal duplications and complex ovarian cysts. [5] The enteric duplications being accountable for a larger portion of the surgical interventions is also in line with the findings in the previous study of Thakkar et al. [23] The favorable prognosis and low surgical rate in our cohort may also partly reflect the focus on solely isolated cases within our cohort, as prior research demonstrated that associated anomalies increase the risk of adverse outcome. [12].
Within this cohort, we found that there was an association between the timing of prenatal diagnosis, with a significantly higher odds of surgical intervention in cases diagnosed before 24 weeks of gestation compared to cases diagnosed after 24 weeks of gestation (< 16 weeks vs. > 24 weeks OR 5.03; 95% CI [1.04–24.42], p = 0.045) (16–24 weeks vs. > 24 weeks OR 6.42, 95% CI [2.49–16.51], p < 0.001). The relatively low surgical rate in cases diagnosed after 24 weeks of gestation (21.3% and 23.8% before 16 and between 16 and 24 weeks of gestation respectively vs. 11.6% after 24 weeks of gestation) could be attributable to the high rate of ovarian cysts in the third trimester, whilst the proportion of persistent ovarian cysts that received surgical treatment was relatively low (11/82, 13.4%). The manifestation of ovarian cysts in the third trimester is thought to be a result of elevated fetal gonadotrophins, placental human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) and maternal estrogens stimulating the fetal ovaries. [2, 5, 19, 32] As spontaneous regression of these cysts has been reported frequently, this might have attributed to the higher proportion of cysts diagnosed after 24 weeks of gestation that received expectative management. In addition, cysts with a maximum prenatal cyst size ≥ 40 mm were more likely to receive surgical intervention (OR 10.25; 95% CI [4.08–25.74], p < 0.001). The association between large cyst diameter and chance of surgical intervention is in line with previous studies [33, 34]; however, larger cysts do not always require surgical intervention and can regress spontaneously. Lastly, gender was not associated with the risk of surgical intervention. Despite the higher rate of female fetuses and neonates within this cohort due to the large proportion of ovarian cysts, the surgical rates were not significantly higher in female neonates compared to male neonates (15.2% vs. 20.4%).

## MAIN FINDINGS
This study demonstrates a high rate of spontaneous regression in cases with isolated fetal abdominal cysts (171/253, 67.6%), of which the majority did not receive surgical treatment (212/253, 83.8%). After birth, the suspected abdominal cyst was reclassified as another anomaly during follow‐up in 3.6% (9/253). Of the cases with suspected prenatal regression, two cases of a transient cyst diagnosed in the first trimester required surgical intervention postpartum. In contrast, we found that cysts diagnosed after 24 weeks are less frequently associated with the need for surgery.

## STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS
This multicenter study evaluated outcomes in a large cohort of isolated fetal abdominal cysts within the Dutch national screening program, where routine third‐trimester ultrasound screening is not routinely performed. Restricting the analysis to isolated fetal abdominal cysts eliminates the possible influence of associated anomalies on prognosis, but therefore provides restricted value for prognostic counseling of non‐isolated cysts. By including cysts diagnosed from the first trimester onwards, we found that cysts diagnosed before 16 weeks of gestation, even if transient, warrant careful postpartum follow‐up. In 38.7% (98/253) of the cases the abdominal cyst was diagnosed before 24 weeks of gestation within the timeframe or prior the second trimester anomaly scan. However, the majority of the fetuses (155/253, 61.3%) with abdominal cysts were detected after 24 weeks of gestation in the (late) second or third trimester. Therefore, the true prevalence of abdominal cysts occurring in the third trimester might be underestimated in the Dutch population, since routine ultrasound examination in the third trimester is not part of standard obstetric care. This may introduce bias, reflected in higher surgical rates for cysts diagnosed in the third trimester, because larger, clinically significant cysts are more likely to be detected. Previous studies have reported an increase in the uptake of abdominal cysts when performing routine ultrasound scanning in the third trimester. [8, 21] Furthermore, because of the retrospective design of this study, specific prenatal diagnoses were missing for a portion of the cases, precluding the assessment of concordance between pre‐ and postnatal diagnoses for these cysts.

## INTERPRETATION
Prenatal regression rates of this cohort of 28.3% are comparable to previous studies that reported spontaneous in utero regression of fetal abdominal cysts in 22%–29%. [22, 23] In this cohort, routine postnatal follow‐up ultrasound for cases with suspected prenatal regression of the cyst was not yet routinely agreed upon because of the low risk of a persistent symptomatic cyst. In 2/53 (3.7%) of the cases with suspected prenatal regression of the cyst, the neonate received surgical treatment postpartum, including one case of anorectal malformation and one torsion of a duplication cyst. Both of these cases were diagnosed with a transient cyst in the first trimester. However, case reports of anorectal malformations after a lower abdominal cyst in the first trimester have been described. [13, 24] Consequently, in prenatally regressing cysts diagnosed in the first trimester, the possibility of a persistent lesion requiring surgical management should be considered.
Of the persistent cysts postpartum, a specific prenatal diagnosis on the origin of the cyst was reported in 106/144. The concordance of the pre‐ and postnatal diagnosis was obtained in 82/106 (77.4%). The highest correspondence between pre‐ and postnatal diagnosis was reported in the persistent ovarian, splenic and hepatic cysts (correspondence 58.8%, 85.7% and 75% respectively). However, definitive conclusions on correspondence rates are more challenging in the cysts with low incidence within the cohort. Catania et al. reported a similar high correspondence rate in the group of simple ovarian cysts, along with high rates of correct diagnosis in the meconium pseudocyst subgroup [5], which were not diagnosed in our cohort. On the contrary, none of the enteric duplications in the current study (28/141 of the persistent cysts 19.9%) received this specific prenatal diagnosis. These cysts were most frequently labeled as non‐specified abdominal cysts, which is in line with findings of previous studies. [1, 5, 22, 25] A possible explanation is the difficulty of defining the typical ‘double wall aspect’ of enteric duplications on prenatal ultrasound. As this is not always (or only partially) identified, other differential diagnoses remain possible. [26] Previous studies have reported an increased prenatal specific diagnostic rate through additional MRI imaging, which was not a standard procedure within our study. [17, 27, 28] Future research should consider the possible prognostic gain of MRI, weighed against the additional costs and outcomes associated with extra imaging, even more so in a relatively benign anomaly such as the fetal abdominal cyst. Lastly, in 9/253 (3.6%), a different anomaly than the abdominal cyst was diagnosed postpartum, which has also been reported in previous studies. [1, 29, 30] Therefore, general evaluation by a pediatric surgeon or pediatric and adolescent gynecologist and simultaneous ultrasound examination in children with prenatally persistent cysts in the first weeks of life will aid in confirmation of the prenatal diagnosis and provide reassurance to parents. Because obtaining a definitive diagnosis prenatally can be challenging, prenatal counseling should address that an alternative diagnosis may arise postpartum as well as the inherent residual risk that certain anomalies may go unseen on prenatal ultrasound.
In total, 16.2% (41/253) of the prenatally identified cysts postnatal surgery was performed. This is relatively low compared to other studies in which postnatal surgery was performed in 29%–81%. [1, 5, 17, 23, 30, 31] Lv et al. included large numbers of choledochal cysts and sacrococcygeal teratomas which required surgery, [17] whereas this study only found choledochal cysts in 6/141 (4.2%) of the persistent cysts and one abdominal teratoma, which may have resulted in lower surgical rates within this cohort. Catania et al. reported the highest percentage of surgical interventions (81%), which was attributed to a relatively high percentage of gastro‐intestinal duplications and complex ovarian cysts. [5] The enteric duplications being accountable for a larger portion of the surgical interventions is also in line with the findings in the previous study of Thakkar et al. [23] The favorable prognosis and low surgical rate in our cohort may also partly reflect the focus on solely isolated cases within our cohort, as prior research demonstrated that associated anomalies increase the risk of adverse outcome. [12].
Within this cohort, we found that there was an association between the timing of prenatal diagnosis, with a significantly higher odds of surgical intervention in cases diagnosed before 24 weeks of gestation compared to cases diagnosed after 24 weeks of gestation (< 16 weeks vs. > 24 weeks OR 5.03; 95% CI [1.04–24.42], p = 0.045) (16–24 weeks vs. > 24 weeks OR 6.42, 95% CI [2.49–16.51], p < 0.001). [2, 5, 19, 32] As spontaneous regression of these cysts has been reported frequently, this might have attributed to the higher proportion of cysts diagnosed after 24 weeks of gestation that received expectative management. ## CONCLUSION
Fetal abdominal cysts have a good prognosis with a high rate of spontaneous prenatal or postnatal regression (67.6%). Surgical intervention (16.2%) was associated with a maximum prenatal cyst > 40 mm and timing of prenatal diagnosis, but not with gender. Of the cases with suspected prenatal regression, two cases of a transient cyst diagnosed in the first trimester required surgical intervention postpartum. Therefore, even when first‐trimester cysts regress prenatally, the potential for a persistent lesion requiring surgical intervention must be acknowledged and structured postnatal follow‐up is recommended.
