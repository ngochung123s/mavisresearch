TITLE: Application of Management, Outcome, Risk, and Expectation Classification for Antenatally Suspected Structural Anomalies: A Multidisciplinary Experience

=== A ===
The study aimed to assess the outcome of antenatally suspected structural congenital anomalies (ASSCAs) using a Management, Outcome, Risk, and Expectation (MORE) framework.
A total of 426 ASSCAs were studied from records of a multidisciplinary clinic from August 2016 to August 2024. After excluding cases requiring initial genetic evaluation, 261 pregnancies underwent MORE analysis.
Maternal age ranged from 25 to 45 years (mean: 35 ± 3). Structural anomalies were first suspected between 12 and 33 weeks; 52.8% were identified before 20 weeks. The M-1, M-2, M-3, M-4, and M-5 groups accounted for 55/261 (21.1%), 159/261 (60.9%), 28/261 (10.7%), 2/261 (0.8%), and 17/261 (6.5%), respectively. Outcomes were O-1, O-2, O-3, O-4, and O-5 in 63/261 (24.1%), 92/261 (35.2%), 83/261 (31.8%), 4/261 (1.5%), and 19/261 (7.3%), respectively. Referral for karyotyping was needed in 83/261 (31.8%) pregnancies (R-2), while R-1 and R-3 accounted for 160/261 (61.3%) and 18/261 (6.9%), respectively. R-2 cases incorporated the presence of more than two soft markers of aneuploidy, multiple anomalies, abdominal wall defects, and facial anomalies, etc., Parental expectations were categorized as E-1, E-2, and E-3 in 7/261 (2.7%), 220/261 (84.3%), and 34/261 (13.0%), respectively. Neonatal surgery was required in 68/261 (24.5%), while eight neonatal deaths occurred due to severe anomalies.

Despite these benefits, its implementation was not uniform across healthcare settings, as attrition, limited availability of advanced diagnostic infrastructure, and sociocultural as well as economic constraints substantially influenced its feasibility and sustainability.


## I
Congenital malformations pose a significant global public health issue, contributing significantly to perinatal and neonatal sickness and death. The estimated prevalence of major congenital anomalies varies depending on surveillance methods: 3%–4% of all neonates are affected in passive surveillance systems and 6%–8% in active surveillance systems worldwide.[12] Major congenital anomalies are the fifth most common cause of neonatal mortalities.[34] Although these figures offer a global perspective, the burden is unevenly distributed, with developing countries bearing over 80% of the world’s population. In these regions, limited healthcare infrastructure, socioeconomic challenges, and restricted access to advanced diagnostics and treatments worsen the outcomes linked to congenital anomalies.
The implications of congenital anomalies extend beyond mere mortality figures. They also cause lifelong disability, emotional and financial burdens on families, and socioeconomic costs to the healthcare system. Major congenital disabilities account for 8% to 15% of perinatal deaths and 13% to 16% of neonatal deaths in India.[5678] Beyond prevention, early detection is vital in shaping neonatal outcomes. Targeted Imaging for Fetal Anomalies and related antenatal imaging techniques can accurately identify a broad spectrum of structural malformations, enabling healthcare providers to counsel families, arrange delivery in appropriate settings, and initiate timely postnatal interventions.[9]
Planned delivery in cases of antenatally suspected structural congenital anomalies (ASSCAs) has consistently been shown to improve neonatal survival by ensuring that the neonate is born in a center equipped with specialized resources for immediate stabilization and surgical or medical intervention. The Management, Outcome, Risk, and Expectation (MORE) classification is a validated counseling and prognostication tool for antenatally detected structural anomalies. Its prospective validation was demonstrated by Agrawal et al., who showed improved uniformity in counseling and outcome prediction across anomaly spectra.[10] This study applies the MORE framework in a real-world, resource-constrained setting without modification, thereby assessing its external applicability in a low- and middle-income country (LMIC) context.[10] The present study aimed to characterize the demographic and clinical profiles of pregnancies with ASSCAs, assess the outcomes using the MORE system, and evaluate postnatal outcomes, including surgical interventions, survival rates, and follow-up patterns.

## M
The study was a retrospective review of medical records collected from a dedicated multidisciplinary ASSCA clinic from August 2016 to August 2025. The clinic functions through the collaboration of specialists from pediatric surgery, neonatology, and obstetrics and gynecology. Given the retrospective nature of data collection and the anonymized analysis, the (IEC) Institute Ethical committee (2024-3-IMP (NF)-9, dated 1.4.25) granted a formal waiver of individual informed consent. Eligible cases were identified from ASSCA clinic registers. Suspicion of ASSCA was primarily based on antenatal ultrasound from the 8th week of gestation through the neonatal period, including targeted antenatal imaging for fetal anomalies. Sonographic results were accompanied by relevant first-trimester combined screening and quad screening. Their utilization was guided by a tiered approach to enhance diagnostic accuracy, minimizing unnecessary invasive procedures in line with the Federation of Obstetric and Gynaecological Societies of India protocols. High risk offered confirmatory diagnostic testing such as chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis; intermediate risk offered quad screening in the second trimester, and low risk not advised to undergo further invasive testing.[1112] Pregnancies lost to follow-up (34.5%, 147/426) were excluded from analyses of outcomes, management, and expectations.

Parents of fetuses with ASSCA received counseling using a nondirective approach. Sessions covered the nature and severity of the anomaly, expected prognosis and possible interventions, risk of recurrence in future pregnancies, and available options, including continuation or medical termination of pregnancy (MTP). Counseling was conducted jointly by obstetricians, neonatologists, and pediatric surgeons to ensure that parents received accurate information about both prenatal and postnatal implications. Records were analyzed to categorize pregnancies using the MORE system, where M (Management) indicates the mode of antenatal care, O (Outcome) concerns pregnancy results and neonatal survival, R (Risk) refers to the chance of recurrence in future pregnancies, and E (Expectation) reflects parental expectations based on counseling.
All neonates delivered at the study center underwent systematic clinical examinations at birth and before hospital discharge. Perinatal outcomes were evaluated based on admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) and the requirement for surgical intervention. The final diagnosis of congenital anomaly was confirmed either by the 28th day of life or at the time of neonatal death. For cases delivered outside the study center, efforts were made to obtain follow-up information through referral records and postnatal clinic visits.
Demographic information, maternal history, details of antenatal screening, anomaly type, management decisions, delivery details, surgical interventions, and neonatal outcomes were systematically collected from hospital records.
The data were analyzed using SPSS for Windows, Version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and range for skewed data. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Comparative analyses were conducted, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## D
In India, congenital anomalies are recognized as a major factor contributing to perinatal and neonatal mortality. It accounts for 8%–15% of perinatal deaths and 13%–16% of neonatal deaths.[81314] The structured screening programs, strong referral systems, widespread access to neonatal surgical facilities, and multidisciplinary management are crucial for improving neonatal outcomes.[1315] In contrast, in LMICs, systemic barriers hinder the integration of these approaches into routine maternal–fetal care, referrals are often delayed, and many women present late in gestation or at the time of delivery, limiting the potential for anticipatory care.[16] India is the second most populous country in the world, with an estimated 27 million births annually; even a modest rate of congenital anomalies results in a substantial total burden.[17] The current rate of congenital abnormalities is a prevalence rate from 61 to 69.9 per 1000 live births.[1819] However, underreporting, inconsistent case definitions, and insufficient surveillance contribute to variability in these estimates. The Birth Defects Registry of India, created under the Fetal Care Research Foundation, has made significant progress in consolidating reports from several states and union territories.[2021] Still, the registry’s estimated prevalence of 84.2 per 10,000 live births in 2010 was much lower than the expected global minimum of 2%, indicating underestimation and gaps in systematic reporting.[1922]
Approximately one-third of pregnancies in our study required referral for genetic evaluation, emphasizing the need to expand access to genetic testing. Early detection before 20 weeks of gestation was achieved in 221/426 (52.8%) of cases, but attrition remained high, with over one-third of pregnancies lost to follow-up. Psychological distress, religious beliefs, sociocultural norms, and economic considerations often influence parental counseling in cases of antenatally suspected anomalies. While the role of healthcare providers is to offer nondirective counseling that explains the nature of the anomaly, possible interventions, and prognosis, the final decision regarding continuation or termination of pregnancy is deeply personal.[21] The importance of ASSCA clinics lies precisely in filling these gaps. The multidisciplinary clinics help counsel and guide parental decision-making for high-risk pregnancies, balancing medical, ethical, and psychosocial considerations in a culturally sensitive manner. By pooling expertise across pediatrics, neonatology, obstetrics and gynecology, pediatric surgery, and genetics, these clinics can offer integrated care pathways for families at risk of having ASSCA pregnancies. They also create an opportunity for standardized data collection, which is vital for developing a clearer epidemiological picture of congenital abnormalities in the Indian context.[23]
The prevalence of genitourinary anomalies in our cohort, 149/426 (34.9%), aligns with the published literature, in which urinary tract dilatation remains among the most common antenatal findings (20%–30%). Higher frequency of congenital CNS anomalies in our series compared to some Western registries may reflect referral bias, as pregnancies with CNS malformations might be referred to neurosurgeons (missing cases) or severe lethal malformations (e.g., anencephaly and holoprosencephaly) are often terminated at peripheral centers before referral.[2425] In our cohort, no women reported preconception folic acid use, a public health concern that highlights the urgent need for widespread nutritional interventions. Early detection before 20 weeks was achieved in just over half of pregnancies, which aligns with global benchmarks (>70%).[2627]
The MORE classification facilitated a structured categorization of cases, although challenges persist in applying this framework to real-world decision-making. For example, while MTP was recommended in 21/426 (4.9%) of cases in our cohort, actual parental decisions were influenced more by sociocultural and financial factors than by clinical advice. Furthermore, implementing these frameworks in LMICs presents challenges due to disparities in diagnostic resources and sociocultural factors that influence parental decision-making. There were always financial, logistical, and acceptance barriers rather than definitional discordance for genetic testing. Similar gaps between medical guidance and parental choices have been observed in South Asian settings, highlighting the complex interaction between medicine, culture, and economics.[15]
CDH accounted for the majority of neonatal surgical interventions, with 14 successful repairs. Survival rates for CDH at our center remain lower than those reported in high-resource countries (over 70%).[1623] The high rate of families lost to follow-up and leaving against medical advice [78/426 (18.3%), and 20/426 (4.7%)] is striking. Financial constraints, lack of insurance coverage, and cultural beliefs about disability all contributed. Similar findings have been reported in multicenter Indian audits, where economic and social determinants strongly influence attrition from neonatal care pathways.[317]
Our findings underscore the importance of structured, empathetic, and culturally sensitive counseling. The divergence between medical advice and parental choices in this study highlights the need for antenatal counseling to extend beyond biomedical risk to address families’ social and economic contexts.[12]
Over 47% of anomalies were identified after 20 weeks of gestation, highlighting the importance of strengthening first-trimester and early second-trimester screening at the community level.[23]
The MORE classification, although useful for categorization, requires adaptation for low- and middle-income settings. For example, the “expectations of parents” domain in our cohort was dominated by financial feasibility rather than strictly medical prognostication. A modified framework that incorporates familial socioeconomic feasibility may improve its utility in LMIC contexts.[9]
The burden of surgically correctable anomalies, particularly CDH, underscores the urgent need to build capacity in neonatal surgery and perioperative care. Investment in pediatric surgical infrastructure and long-term rehabilitation services is essential if survival rates are to approach those of high-resource countries.[18]
The complete lack of preconception of folic acid use among all mothers in our study was a significant finding. Preconception counseling campaigns could prevent a substantial proportion of anomalies.[18]
The limited uptake of genetic testing highlights inequities in access. Expanding government-subsidized microarray services, particularly in public hospitals, would enable earlier and more equitable detection of abnormalities.[15]
Attrition from follow-up highlights broader challenges in ongoing care. Creating centralized birth defect registries and linking antenatal, perinatal, and neonatal care through digital health platforms, telemedicine, and mobile health applications could reduce loss to follow-up and enhance outcome monitoring. Some structural anomalies may require staged or delayed treatment, emphasizing the need for long-term follow-up and ongoing multidisciplinary support to optimize functional outcomes and minimize long-term developmental, surgical, and psychosocial consequences for affected children.
The study’s extensive data collection over several years enhances the reliability of the findings and enables the identification of temporal trends. The multidisciplinary clinic model ensures a comprehensive approach to diagnosis, counseling, and postnatal follow-up.
The study emphasizes practical challenges such as financial constraints, limited access to advanced diagnostic tools, and loss to follow-up, reflecting real-world clinical situations. The findings might not be fully generalizable to the broader Indian population, especially in rural areas where healthcare resources are even scarcer. Being a retrospective analysis, the study is prone for reporting and referral bias. The reliance on ultrasonography for antenatal diagnosis is another limitation, as anomaly detection rates vary significantly by GA, operator skill, and machine quality. Follow-up duration was inconsistent, with substantial attrition, hampering long-term outcome assessment. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable baseline data and highlights the need for multicentric prospective registries and improved antenatal care infrastructure across India.
The findings are expected to assist caregivers, policymakers, and families in setting realistic expectations about prognosis, emphasize the importance of multidisciplinary antenatal care, and advocate for strengthening maternal–child health systems. Incorporating standardized protocols for risk stratification and counseling, such as the MORE classification, into national antenatal care guidelines may help ensure consistent care across centers.

## C
While early detection and multidisciplinary care enable favorable outcomes in selected cases, systemic barriers – including delayed diagnosis, financial constraints, limited genetic testing, and neonatal surgical capacity – continue to shape prognosis. Multidisciplinary ASSCA clinics can apply standardized frameworks, such as MORE, thereby optimizing prognostication, guiding informed parental decision-making, and enhancing neonatal care. Beyond clinical care, culturally sensitive counseling and sustained follow-up systems are essential to address the socioeconomic realities of affected families.
