TITLE: Comparative efficacy of human chorionic gonadotropin alone versus dual trigger for oocyte maturation in advanced maternal age using the antagonist protocol: a randomized controlled trial

=== BACKGROUND ===
The dual trigger, combining a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist with hCG, is reported to improve number of mature oocytes, and cumulative live birth rates. However, few studies have focused on advanced-aged women, and the relationship between follicle size and MII oocyte retrieval rates in this group remains unclear.
This randomized controlled trial enrolled 164 patients aged 35–42 years undergoing intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). Participants were randomized (1:1) to hCG-only or dual trigger. Final oocyte maturation was induced when ≥ 2 follicles reached 17 mm, with retrieval 35 h later. Oocytes were aspirated by follicle size (< 13 mm, 13–17 mm, > 17 mm). All mature oocytes underwent ICSI. Embryo transfer was performed in fresh or frozen-thawed cycles. The primary outcome was MII oocytes count; secondary outcomes were fertilization, implantation, pregnancy, live birth, and miscarriage rates.
Among 164 participants, 83 were in the hCG group and 81 in the dual trigger group. Baseline characteristics, ovarian response, and types and doses of stimulation medicines were statistically comparable. The mean number of MII oocytes was 6.07 ± 4.02 in the hCG group and 5.78 ± 3.62 in the dual trigger group (P = 0.62). Fertilization rates were similar between groups. Subgroup analysis by follicle size (< 13 mm, 13–17 mm, and > 17 mm) showed that follicles < 13 mm in the dual trigger group produced more mature oocytes and a significantly higher fertilization rate compared with hCG-only (40.71% vs. 32.65%, P = 0.08; 80.43% vs. 65.25%, P < 0.03). The implantation rate, chemical pregnancy rate, and clinical pregnancy rate were clinically higher in the dual trigger group (37.7% vs. 28.57%; 45.90% vs. 34.92%; 40.98% vs. 34.92%), although the differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.31, 0.27, 0.58, respectively). Ongoing pregnancy and live birth rates remained similar (P = 0.84).
In advanced-aged patients, the dual trigger and hCG trigger protocols yielded comparable numbers of total oocytes and MII oocytes. However, subgroup analysis indicated that dual trigger may improve fertilization rates in smaller follicles. Although a trend toward higher chemical and clinical pregnancy rates was observed with dual trigger, ongoing pregnancy and live birth rates remained comparable between groups.
This study was registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (https://www.thaiclinicaltrials.org) on 15 March 2022 under the registration number TCTR20220315002.

## BACKGROUND
Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) has been used for decades to induce oocyte maturation in conventional ovarian hyperstimulation cycles. However, in natural cycles, oocyte maturation results from a luteinizing hormone (LH) surge accompanied by a mild rise in follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) during midcycle. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRHa) trigger simulates this natural physiology [1, 2] by inducing concurrent LH and FSH surges, which peak at 4 and 8 h after administration, respectively [3]. FSH stimulates the luteinizing hormone receptor (LHR) mRNA expression in mural granulosa cells, thus increase the LH receptors [4, 5]. Early human studies showed that GnRHa trigger alone could reduce ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome and increase metaphase II (MII) oocyte yields [6]. However, subsequent investigations found GnRHa trigger alone less effective than hCG [7]. In addition, GnRHa trigger alone in fresh cycles has been linked to lower implantation and clinical pregnancy rates, potentially due to luteal phase insufficiency [8]. A recent meta-analysis reported that certain patient groups, particularly those with higher body mass index or lower ovarian reserve, had suboptimal responses to GnRHa trigger compared with hCG trigger [9]. By contrast, concomitant administration of GnRHa and hCG for final oocyte maturation yielded more favorable outcomes, including higher oocyte retrieval, implantation, pregnancy, and live birth rates in both fresh and frozen embryo transfer cycles, and even the poor responders [10–12].
Dual trigger, which combines GnRHa and hCG, has been extensively studied in patients with varying ovarian responses, including high [13, 14], normal [13, 15–18], and poor [10, 12, 19–24] responders. Many studies have demonstrated improved oocyte and MII retrieval, as well as better pregnancy and live birth rates, with dual trigger compared to hCG alone. Several meta-analyses have also favored dual trigger [11, 25–27]. However, the type and dosage of hCG and GnRHa have differed among studies. For example, investigators have used triptorelin at 0.1‒0.4 mg [28–30], buserelin at 0.5 mg [6], leuprolide at 1 mg [31], and various doses of urinary or recombinant hCG [28, 29, 31]. One study found comparable outcomes among triptorelin 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4 mg [30]. Most published trials enrolled predominantly younger patients [31–35]. Only a few studies have focused on advanced-aged women or included subgroup analyses for this population [16, 36–38]. Many of these retrospective investigations may have selection bias or data collection challenges, affecting their capacity to assess the impact of dual trigger in older patients. Furthermore, these studies included local patients of a different ethnicity.
Advanced maternal age is commonly defined as 35 years or older [39]. Achieving pregnancy at this age often requires retrieving a higher number of oocytes because embryos are more prone to aneuploidy [40]. A larger oocyte pool increases the likelihood of obtaining at least one euploid embryo. Additionally, there is a significant positive correlation between maternal age and the number of immature (metaphase I [MI]) oocytes retrieved [41], especially in women aged 40 years or older [42]. However, current evidence remains insufficient to confirm whether dual trigger enhances the number of mature oocytes in this older demographic.
Based on favorable outcomes reported in general patient groups and in poor responders, we hypothesized that dual trigger could produce more mature oocytes than hCG alone in advanced-aged women. Therefore, we conducted this study to compare oocyte maturation and retrieval rates between dual trigger and hCG-alone triggers in advanced maternal age using an antagonist protocol. Our secondary objective was to compare fertilization rates, implantation rates, and pregnancy outcomes between the two groups. We also evaluated whether reproductive outcomes differed according to follicle size (> 17 mm, 13–17 mm, and < 13 mm).

## METHODS
The study protocol was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, and was registered at https://www.thaiclinicaltrials.org (TCTR20220315002). The randomized controlled trial was conducted between July 2022 and April 2024 at the Infertility and Reproductive Biology Unit, Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Faculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital. All participants were informed and provided written consent. The study included Thai infertile women aged 35‒42 years who were indicated for intracytoplasmic sperm injection cycles using an antagonist protocol for ovarian stimulation.
The exclusion criteria encompassed risk factors for ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, including a history of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, an antral follicle count ≥ 24, anti-Müllerian hormone ≥ 3.5 ng/mL, or ≥ 25 follicles on the trigger day. Patients with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, a large endometrioma (> 5 cm), a body mass index > 30 kg/m², or those who required surgically retrieved sperm were also excluded.
All participants were divided into two groups by a computer-generated block randomization of four, using a 1:1 allocation ratio. Before the study, the randomization code was sealed in an opaque envelope by a research nurse. On the first day of ovarian stimulation, another research nurse opened the envelope and assigned the patient to the designated group. The attending physician, embryologist, and statistician were blinded to the randomization results. Patients in the study group received a dual trigger consisting of simultaneous injections of 0.25 mg hCG (Ovidrel; Merck Serono, Bari, Italy) and 0.2 mg GnRHa (triptorelin acetate; Decapeptyl; Ferring Pharmaceutical, Saint-Prex, Switzerland). Patients in the control group received 0.25 mg hCG alone.
On Days 1‒3 of the menstrual cycle, blood tests were performed to measure baseline LH, FSH, estradiol (E2), and anti-Müllerian hormone. Antral follicles were counted using transvaginal ultrasonography (Xario 100; Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). All participants underwent individualized controlled ovarian stimulation using recombinant or urinary gonadotropins (Gonal-F [Merck Serono Ltd, Darmstadt, Germany], Pergoveris [Merck Serono Ltd], or IVF-M [LG Chem Ltd, Seoul, South Korea]). The dosage was adjusted according to the ovarian response.
A GnRH antagonist, ganirelix (Orgalutran; Organon, Ravensburg, Germany), was administered starting on Day 6 of stimulation to prevent a premature LH surge. Follicle diameters were assessed regularly by transvaginal ultrasonography. Oocyte maturation was triggered when at least two leading follicles reached ≥ 17 mm in diameter. Participants received either hCG alone or a dual trigger in accordance with their assigned group. Oocyte retrieval was scheduled 35 h after triggering. The risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome was evaluated before triggering. Participants who developed more than 25 growing follicles were withdrawn from the study, received GnRHa for triggering only, and had all embryos cryopreserved.
Oocyte aspiration was performed by one physician, using a single-lumen needle (Kitazato®) under ultrasound guidance with an aspiration pressure of 120 mmHg. Follicle sizes were measured immediately before aspiration, and the number of punctured follicles was recorded. Follicles were categorized by size (> 17 mm, 13–17 mm, or < 13 mm), aspirated sequentially, and stored in tubes labeled with color-coded stickers.
All retrieved oocytes were cultured in Universal IVF Medium (Cooper Surgical Fertility Solutions, Malov, Denmark) at 37 °C with 5% O₂ and 6% CO₂ for 4 h before classification. Oocytes were arranged according to follicle size. MII oocytes were identified by the presence of the first polar body in the perivitelline space. MI oocytes showed no visible germinal vesicle or first polar body. Germinal vesicle oocytes contained an eccentrically located nucleus with a distinct single nucleolus [43–45]. After cumulus cell removal, MII oocytes were inseminated by intracytoplasmic sperm injection under confocal microscope (Nikon, Eclipse Ti2, Japan). Fertilization was confirmed 18 h later by the appearance of two pronuclei.
All fertilized oocytes were incubated in Sydney IVF Cleavage Medium (K-SICM-50; Cook Medical Inc, Bloomington, IN, USA). Some cleavage-stage embryos were transferred in fresh cycles, while others were cryopreserved. The remaining embryos were further cultured in Sydney IVF Blastocyst Medium (K-SIBM-50; Cook Medical Inc) until the blastocyst stage. Patients requiring preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy had their blastocysts biopsied and then cryopreserved. The biopsied genetic material was analyzed by next-generation sequencing.
During frozen–thaw cycles, embryos were warmed according to procedures described in our previous study [46].
To thaw Day 3 embryos, the thawing media was first prewarmed in an incubator at 37 °C. Vitrified embryos were removed from liquid nitrogen, and the outer straw was immediately detached. The inner straw contents were then gently poured into thaw medium 1, which contained 1 M sucrose in PBS. After 60 s in thaw medium 1, the embryos were transferred to thaw medium 2 (0.5 M sucrose in PBS) for 3 min. Next, they were placed in thaw medium 3 (PBS only) for 5 min. The Day 3 embryos were then cultured in Sydney IVF Blastocyst Medium (K-SIBM-50, Cook Medical Inc) at 37 °C under 5% O₂ and 6% CO₂ for approximately 2 h before transfer.
To thaw blastocysts, embryos were placed in thaw medium 1 (0.5 M sucrose in DPBS) at 37 °C for 3 min. They were then transferred to thaw medium 2 (0.25 M sucrose in DPBS) and thaw medium 3 (0.125 M sucrose in DPBS), spending three minutes in each solution. Finally, the blastocysts were cultured in Sydney IVF Blastocyst Medium (K-SIBM-50, Cook Medical, Inc., Bloomington, IN, USA) at 37 °C under 5% O₂ and 6% CO₂ for about 2 h prior to transfer.
Patients who received fresh embryo transfer had a low number of mature oocytes available and demonstrated favorable endometrial characteristics on ultrasonography. In contrast, patients requiring preimplantation genetic testing, presenting with thin endometrial lining on ultrasonography, undergoing oocyte retrieval transversing uterus, or possessing an adequate number of embryos were scheduled for frozen embryo transfer. In each frozen–thaw cycle, a daily oral dose of 4 mg estradiol hemihydrate (Estrofem; Novo Nordisk A/S, Bagsværd, Denmark) was started on Day 2 of the menstrual cycle. The dosage was adjusted based on endometrial thickness, which was measured regularly by transvaginal ultrasound. An endometrial thickness ≥ 7 mm with a triple-layer appearance qualified for embryo transfer. At that stage, intravaginal micronized progesterone (Utrogestan; OLIC, Phra Nakhon Si Ayutthaya, Thailand) 600 mg per day was prescribed for 3‒5 days before embryo transfer, depending on the day of the transferred embryos. One or two cleavage-stage embryos or blastocysts were transferred under transabdominal ultrasound guidance using a soft catheter (Gynetics, Lommel, Belgium). Serum beta hCG was measured 14 days after embryo transfer.
A biochemical pregnancy was defined as beta hCG > 25 IU/mL [46]. Clinical pregnancy was confirmed by transvaginal ultrasound demonstrating an intrauterine gestational sac 2 weeks later. Ongoing pregnancy was defined as a viable pregnancy at 12 weeks of gestation, and live birth was defined as a live delivery after 24 weeks of gestation. Miscarriage was defined as a pregnancy loss occurring in the first trimester.
From data reviewed at Siriraj Hospital, the mean (SD) number of MII oocytes retrieved after an hCG trigger in infertile women aged 35‒42 years was 7 ± 4.5, while the mean of oocyte retrieved following a dual trigger was 9. Therefore, 80 participants per group were required, with an additional 5% loss yielding a total of 168 participants. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). The demographic and baseline data of the patients were represented using mean and standard deviation for normally distributed variables; otherwise, median and interquartile range were employed. Comparative analysis between groups was conducted utilizing Student’s t-test and chi-squared test, as appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Subgroup analysis was initially planned for each original follicular size category.

## RESULTS
A total of 420 women were assessed for eligibility, and 168 eligible participants were recruited and randomized (Fig. 1). Four participants withdrew because of concerns about ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, a decision to discontinue participation, or an oocyte retrieval canceled for nonmedical reasons. The remaining 164 participants were assigned to the hCG group (n = 83) or the dual trigger group (n = 81).

Embryo transfers were performed for 63 patients in the hCG group and 61 in the dual trigger group. Most patients (88.9% in the hCG group and 86.9% in the dual trigger group) underwent embryo transfer during frozen-thaw cycles. Some patients underwent preimplantation genetic testing but did not identify any euploid embryos for transfer. Reasons for no embryo transfer during the study period appear in Fig. 1. The fertilization rates were comparable in both groups (Table 3). However, subgroup analysis revealed a significantly higher fertilization rate among MII oocytes from smaller follicles in the dual trigger group than in the hCG group (P = 0.03; Fig. 2). The implantation rates, chemical and clinical pregnancy rates tended to be higher in the dual trigger group (37.70 ± 44.40 vs. 28.57 ± 41.85; 45.9% vs. 34.92%; 40.98% vs. 34.92%), although this difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The ongoing pregnancy and live birth rates were also similar between groups (27.87% vs. 30.16%, P > 0.05) (Table 5). The dual trigger group had a significantly higher miscarriage rate (P = 0.02). After logistic regression to adjust for confounding factors, the dual trigger remained an independent factor (Table 6).

## DISCUSSION
Our study, which recruited participants aged 35‒42 years, found no significant difference in oocyte retrieval rates between the hCG-only group and the dual-trigger group. The overall oocyte maturation rate was also similar between groups. These findings align with the study of Zhou et al. [47], which included an Asian cohort with a mean age of 38 years. However, our results differ from earlier studies involving younger populations [31, 32, 34]. This discrepancy may reflect age-related factors, such as diminished ovarian reserve or anti-Müllerian hormone levels, as well as reduced oocyte maturation capacity in older patients [41]. Additional mechanisms include variations in FSH glycosylation, different LH isoforms, and altered ligand bias in the FSH receptor. Women over 35 years secrete more fully glycosylated FSH variants (FSH24), which have lower affinity for FSH receptors [48–50]. Consequently, the marginal FSH surge after a Gandha trigger may be insufficient to promote oocyte maturation. Older women also secrete LH isoforms that are more sialylated and less sulfated, leading to diminished LH bioactivity. Hence, any additional FSH and LH surge from a dual trigger may not substantially increase oocyte yields in this age group.
Moreover, ligand bias at the FSH receptor may further influence these outcomes. Research indicates that both small-molecule ligands and FSH glycosylation variants can induce ligand bias [51]. In the context of ERK1/2 activation—which mediates oocyte maturation—FSH18 and FSH21 are more potent, whereas FSH24 is least effective [52]. Therefore, older women may not achieve the anticipated improvement in oocyte maturation, even with a dual-trigger approach.
A subgroup analysis of three distinct follicular sizes showed a trend toward increased oocyte maturation rates in small follicles (< 13 mm) in the dual-trigger group, although this was not statistically significant. The fertilization rate also tended to be higher in the dual-trigger group. Notably, MII oocytes retrieved from small follicles in the dual-trigger group exhibited a significantly higher fertilization rate compared with the hCG-only group. During folliculogenesis, large follicles primarily depend on LH, whereas small antral follicles rely more on FSH [53, 54]. Consequently, an additional rise in FSH may directly support cytoplasmic maturation and indirectly promote nuclear maturation by enhancing LH receptor expression in granulosa cells of small follicles [53, 55]. These effects may explain the higher oocyte maturation and fertilization rates observed in small follicles in the dual-trigger group. However, because fewer oocytes are retrieved from these smaller follicles, the overall impact on total maturation and fertilization outcomes remains limited.
The implantation rate was similar in both groups, although the chemical and clinical pregnancy rates tended to be higher in the dual-trigger group. This finding aligns with earlier studies in younger or advanced-age cohorts [34, 35, 38]. However, the live birth rate did not differ significantly between our two groups. Interestingly, the dual-trigger group showed a higher rate of pregnancy loss. The GnRHa in a dual-trigger regimen may enhance endometrial receptivity [38, 56], which can improve implantation and decrease miscarriage, especially in fresh embryo transfer cycles. Nonetheless, most transfers in the present study were frozen embryo transfers, which diminishes the likelihood of detecting the dual trigger’s full benefits on implantation and miscarriage. Several studies have revealed varied abortion outcomes between trigger methods, with some noting higher abortion rates in dual-trigger protocols [32, 57], especially in poor and high responders [57], and others reporting similar or reduced rates [22, 34, 38]. In our study, all abortion cases involved embryos from large-follicle MII oocytes that were mostly untested for genetic abnormalities. Aneuploidy, which is more common in older women, is thus the probable cause of the observed miscarriages. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the factors contributing to higher abortion rates in dual-trigger protocols.
This randomized study in advanced-aged women provides important insights into the dual-trigger method. Stratifying oocyte retrieval by follicle size revealed differences in oocyte maturation and fertilization rates among three follicle-size groups. However, because it was a single-blind study, participants were aware of their group assignment. While the main focus of this study was on the quantity of mature oocytes collected, an objective measure, a double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial could provide more robust evidence. In addition, not all embryos were cultured to the cleavage or blastocyst stage; many were cryopreserved at the two-pronuclear stage, making embryo-quality comparisons unfeasible. Preimplantation testing led to several cycles with no embryos available for transfer, which could introduce bias when analyzing pregnancy outcomes. Serum progesterone levels may also influence pregnancy and miscarriage rates, but these were not measured. Consequently, the absence of progesterone data may have limited our ability to determine the precise causes of miscarriage. This is a single center RCT. It is well-suited for achieving accurate results based on the objective. As an early RCT comparing hCG and dual trigger outcomes in older women, further studies across other ethnicities and countries that had different practice are needed to strengthen future meta-analysis results.

## CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, the MII and oocyte retrieval rates did not differ significantly between the dual-trigger and hCG-only groups in advanced-aged women. Subgroup analysis by follicle size showed comparable MII oocyte counts in both groups. However, MII oocytes retrieved from smaller follicles in the dual-trigger group had a higher fertilization rate than those in the hCG-only group. Despite similar overall fertilization rates, the dual-trigger group showed higher chemical and clinical pregnancy rates. Nevertheless, its miscarriage rate was also significantly higher, leading to comparable ongoing pregnancy and live birth rates between the two groups.
