TITLE: Cytomegalovirus hepatitis in early pregnancy: A case report

=== ABSTRACT ===
Cytomegalovirus (CMV) is a ubiquitous herpesvirus infecting a large proportion of the population worldwide. Clinical manifestations range from asymptomatic infection to severe disease with multi-organ involvement, depending primarily on host immune status. CMV hepatitis is an uncommon clinical presentation. In pregnancy, infection is usually asymptomatic in immunocompetent women; however, transplacental transmission may occur, leading to congenital infection. Congenital CMV is the most common congenital viral infection. This report describes the case of a 30-year-old pregnant woman with acute CMV infection acquired during the first trimester. The disease manifested as hepatitis, with ALT and AST levels reaching up to 31-fold and 15-fold above the upper limits of normal, respectively. Gradual improvement was observed with supportive treatment, and the outcome was favourable for both mother and child. The infant was delivered at term and has shown normal development; at 8 years of age, the only reported condition is lactose intolerance.
Keywords: Cytomegalovirus, Pregnancy, CMV hepatitis, Congenital infection

=== HIGHLIGHTS ===
In immunocompetent pregnant women, CMV hepatitis is rare.
No antiviral therapy was used, patient healed with supportive care only.
Baby was born healthy and developed normally.
Early CMV screening in pregnancy could help to prevent transmission.


## INTRODUCTION
Cytomegalovirus (CMV) is human herpesvirus type 5, first isolated in 1956. The name derives from its ability to cause enlargement of infected cells, resulting in characteristic inclusion bodies visible on microscopic examination [1]. Transmission occurs via body fluids, including saliva, urine, tears, blood, and genital secretions. The prevalence of anti-CMV IgG seropositivity in the general population is high, ranging from 40% to 100% [2], [3]. It is higher in regions with lower socioeconomic status and among older individuals and is not dependent on climatic conditions [2], [4].
Clinical manifestations of acute CMV infection range from asymptomatic infection to severe disease with organ involvement. Disease severity depends primarily on the host's immune status. In immunocompetent individuals, infection is usually asymptomatic or presents as an infectious mononucleosis-like syndrome with fever, lymphocytosis, and atypical lymphocytes. After primary infection, the virus persists in a latent form; however, in immunocompromised individuals, reactivation or primary infection may lead to severe disease, with viremia and organ involvement, associated with high morbidity and mortality. Common organ-specific manifestations include colitis, pneumonia, encephalitis, retinitis, hepatitis, myocarditis, and haemolytic anaemia. Schattner et al. demonstrated that CMV can cause a wide range of clinical syndromes even in immunocompetent individuals [5].
Infection in immunocompetent pregnant women is usually asymptomatic; however, transplacental transmission and congenital infection may occur. Congenital CMV infection is the most common congenital infection, affecting 0.5–2% of live births [6], [7]. Routine serological screening for CMV is not currently recommended in Slovakia or most European countries, although it is still performed in clinical practice.
Few cases of acute CMV hepatitis in immunocompetent individuals have been described, typically with a favourable prognosis, either with antiviral therapy such as (val)ganciclovir [8], [9], [10] or without it [11], [12]. Reactivation of latent infection presenting as hepatitis in immunocompetent patients is less common [13]. Acute CMV hepatitis during pregnancy is rare [14], [15]. A case of acute CMV hepatitis in a patient in the first trimester of pregnancy with a favourable outcome is presented here.

## CASE PRESENTATION
A 30-year-old previously healthy nulliparous pregnant woman was admitted to the department of infectious diseases, due to elevated liver enzymes at 11 weeks of gestation. She had no history of chronic illness or regular medication prior to pregnancy. The only medication she used was folic acid.
The patient had been hospitalised earlier, at 5 weeks of gestation, due to bleeding. At that time, ALT was borderline elevated (0.64 μkat/L; normal range 0.2–0.6), while other liver enzymes were within normal limits. During routine prenatal screening at 9 weeks, elevated liver enzymes were detected: AST 4.7, ALT 8.07, GGT 1.21, and ALP 2.34 μkat/L. Blood count, including differential leucogram, was normal. Anti-CMV IgM positivity with IgG negativity was detected. Mild respiratory symptoms, including cough and low-grade fever, were present.
Ten days later, further elevation of liver enzymes was observed, and hospitalisation followed. On admission, only low-grade fever was reported. Epidemiological history revealed no significant risk factors.
Physical examination showed low-grade fever (37.4 °C), tachycardia (111 bpm), mild lymphadenopathy, and splenomegaly. The liver was not enlarged. The remainder of the examination was unremarkable.
Supportive therapy was initiated, including intravenous fluids, vitamins, and hepatoprotective agents. Despite treatment, liver enzymes increased further, to a maximum value of AST 8.83 and ALT 18.61 μkat/l, followed by gradual improvement after two weeks. Bilirubin remained only borderline elevated. The trend of serum AST and ALT levels during the course of hospitalisation demonstrated initial elevation followed by gradual normalization.
Serological testing performed the day after admission confirmed both anti-CMV IgM and IgG positivity, and CMV DNA was detected in blood. The diagnostic protocol available at the time of patient management involved only qualitative PCR testing (detected/not detected). The diagnosis of acute CMV infection was established based on IgM positivity, IgG seroconversion, and CMV DNA detection. Follow-up testing six months later demonstrated IgM negativity with increasing IgG titres (119 IU/ml). Other viral hepatitis markers were negative. Ultrasound examination revealed splenomegaly and cholelithiasis without biliary obstruction.
After discharge, follow-up was uneventful, with normalization of laboratory parameters within four months. Amniocentesis was declined by the patient. Serial monthly foetal ultrasound examinations were normal.
A healthy female infant was delivered at 39 weeks of gestation. Neonatal evaluation showed no abnormalities. The anti-CMV antibodies from the umbilical cord were positive in class IgG and negative in class IgM. The CMV DNA PCR test was not performed. The screening examination of hearing and vision was without pathology. The child went on to develop normally; at 8 years of age, lactose intolerance was the only reported condition.

## DISCUSSION
Symptomatic CMV infection in immunocompetent pregnant women is rare. This report describes acute CMV infection with significant hepatic involvement in early pregnancy (AST and ALT were elevated to 14.7-fold and 31-fold above the upper limit of normal, respectively). The diagnosis was confirmed by CMV DNA positivity and seroconversion of IgG antibodies.
At the time of management, antiviral therapy for the treatment of CMV infection in pregnancy was not recommended. Ganciclovir and valganciclovir are effective but potentially teratogenic [16] and remain controversial during pregnancy. These drugs have been administered to pregnant women only in rare situations, mostly in symptomatic women with immunodeficiency [17], [18], although the results of an observational study [19] show that tertiary prevention with valganciclovir appears to be effective and well tolerated by both foetus and mother. Nevertheless, larger studies accompanied by long-term follow-up are needed. The use of ganciclovir and valganciclovir for the treatment of CMV infection in pregnancy is not recommended [20], [21]. In recent years, the European congenital infection initiative recommends valaciclovir at a high dose of 8 g daily for the treatment of primary CMV infection [21]. Based on the results of several studies and meta-analysis, the treatment with 8 g of valaciclovir significantly reduced vertical transmission, neonatal infection and termination of pregnancy [22], [23], [24], [25].
Despite the absence of antiviral therapy, no congenital infection occurred in the neonate in the present case. Negative anti-CMV IgM antibodies from the umbilical cord excluded foetal CMV infection. The main factor predicting vertical transmission and long-term consequences in infected neonates is gestational age and not severity of the maternal infection [26]. In women with primary infection in the first trimester, the risk of foetal harm is reported to be as high as 23% [27]. Although the infection in the present case occurred in the first trimester, liver enzyme elevation was marked and management was only supportive, no evidence of vertical transmission was observed. Improvement of the liver enzyme activity occurred only very slowly, but the outcome was favourable for both mother and child.
Since an effective treatment to prevent vertical transmission is now available, the introduction of mandatory CMV screening at the beginning of pregnancy may be worth considering.

## CONCLUSION
A rare case of CMV hepatitis in an immunocompetent pregnant patient is presented. Despite significant liver enzyme elevation, the outcome was favourable with supportive treatment. Disease severity does not necessarily correlate with the risk of congenital infection. With the availability of effective treatment options, CMV screening in pregnancy may be reconsidered.
