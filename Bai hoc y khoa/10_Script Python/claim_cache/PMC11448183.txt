TITLE: Prenatal ultrasound detection of fetal amniotic band ingestion in monochorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy: a rare case report

=== ABSTRACT ===
Amniotic Band Syndrome (ABS) is a rare congenital condition characterized by the formation of fibrous bands within the amniotic sac that can entangle and restrict fetal development, leading to various deformities. In this report, we present an unprecedented case of fetal amniotic band ingestion in a monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twin pregnancy. Reporting this case is essential for expanding the understanding of the diverse presentations of amniotic bands, considering this rare entity in the differential diagnosis of prenatal ultrasound findings, and emphasizing the importance of vigilant prenatal monitoring and individualized management strategies.
A 35-year-old gravida 2, para 1 woman with an MCDA twin pregnancy experienced an uneventful pregnancy until the 33rd week, when routine ultrasonography revealed mild intrauterine growth restriction in both twins. At 35 weeks and 5 days gestation, the patient presented with mild abdominal pain. An emergency ultrasound revealed that Twin A ingested an amniotic band. Despite the absence of overt fetal distress, a comprehensive risk assessment was conducted, taking into account potential complications, such as airway obstruction and gastrointestinal issues due to prolonged amniotic band ingestion. Considering the risks versus the benefits of continued gestation and the near-term status, an emergency cesarean section was performed. The procedure resulted in the successful delivery of both twins with satisfactory Apgar scores. A 9-cm fibrous strand was promptly extracted from Twin A’s oral cavity after delivery, confirming prenatal diagnosis. Both neonates underwent thorough examinations, revealing no additional anomalies, and demonstrated normal development during the nine-month follow-up.
This case highlights the inherent challenges in diagnosing and managing the rare presentations of amniotic band-related complications, particularly in MCDA twin pregnancies. The instance of fetal amniotic band ingestion underscores the vital role of comprehensive prenatal imaging in ensuring accurate diagnosis and tailoring individualized risk assessments throughout the pregnancy in such rare and complex situations.
Keywords: Amniotic band syndrome, Fetal ingestion, Monochorionic diamniotic pregnancy, Ultrasonography, Prenatal monitoring, Emergency cesarean section


## BACKGROUND
Amniotic Band Syndrome (ABS) is a rare and complex congenital condition characterized by the formation of fibrous bands in the amniotic sac, which can entangle and constrict various fetal parts, leading to a spectrum of deformities and complications [1, 2]. The incidence of ABS is reported to range between 1 in 1,200 and 1 in 15,000 live births​. The pathogenesis of ABS remains a subject of debate, with two primary hypotheses proposed: the intrinsic theory suggests that the syndrome results from early disruptions in the embryonic development of the amniotic cavity, whereas the extrinsic theory attributes the condition to mechanical disruption of the amniotic membrane, leading to the formation of fibrous bands [3, 4]​.
The presence of ABS poses distinct challenges in pregnancies with monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twins, which share a single chorion but develop in separate amniotic sacs. These pregnancies already have an elevated risk of complications, such as twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS), selective intrauterine growth restriction (sIUGR), and twin anemia-polycythemia sequence, which complicate clinical management when ABS is involved [5–7]. ABS typically manifests as limb constriction, amputation, and other abnormalities caused by fibrous amniotic bands [8]. However, there have been reported cases of fetal ingestion of amniotic bands, a rare presentation that differs from the typical manifestations of ABS and necessitates consideration in the differential diagnosis.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of fetal ingestion of an amniotic band in monochorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy. This study contributes to a better understanding of the clinical complexities and implications of this rare presentation.

## CASE PRESENTATION
We present the case of a 35-year-old gravida 2, para 1 female carrying monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twins, both female. The patient had a medical history of hypothyroidism, which was well-controlled with levothyroxine. Her pregnancy was uneventful until the 33rd week, when a routine ultrasound examination at our hospital raised concerns. Prior to this, all antenatal check-ups and screenings were normal, with no indications of fetal abnormalities or pregnancy-related complications.
During the 33rd-week of ultrasound, both twins were found to have weights and abdominal circumferences below the 10th percentile for gestational age, with estimated weights of 1800 g for Twin A and 1700 g for Twin B. Additionally, the fetal umbilical artery Doppler showed an increased pulsatility index above the 95th percentile, indicating placental insufficiency, although no diastolic flow reversal was observed. The middle cerebral and uterine arteries in both twins exhibited normal resistance and pulsatility indices, with no diastolic flow reversal. Consequently, mild intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) was diagnosed in both fetuses. Importantly, there were no signs of twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS) or selective fetal growth restriction. The patient was advised regular Doppler monitoring [9].
At 35 weeks and 5 days of gestation, the patient presented to the emergency department with mild abdominal pain that had started a few hours earlier. She denied any vaginal bleeding, discharge, fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, or urinary symptoms. Her vital signs were stable, and the physical examination was unremarkable. An emergency two-dimensional ultrasound revealed that Twin A was swallowing an amniotic band, which was visibly extending from the fetus’s mouth (Fig. 1). The estimated fetal weights were 2200 g for Twin A and 2090 g for Twin B.
Ultrasound images of a monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twin pregnancy at 35 weeks and 5 days of gestation. (a-b) Transabdominal images depicting the uterus in both the sagittal and oblique sagittal planes, revealing an observed disruption of the amniotic band ingested by twin A
Given the unique situation where Twin A was observed swallowing an amniotic band, and considering that the pregnancy was nearing full term, the medical team decided to proceed with an emergency cesarean section. Although there was no direct evidence of fetal distress, the unusual circumstances warranted a cautious approach to ensure the safe delivery of both twins.
The cesarean section was performed under spinal anesthesia without any maternal or fetal complications. Twin A was delivered first, with a 9-cm fibrous strand protruding from the mouth, which was immediately and gently extracted (Fig. 2). Both twins had normal Apgar scores of 8 and 9 at 1 and 5 min, respectively.
Postnatal photographs depicting (a) Twin A swallowing an amniotic string and (b) removing a 9-cm fibrous band from the infant’s mouth intraoperatively
Following delivery, both neonates underwent thorough physical examinations, which revealed no additional anomalies or deformities related to ABS. They were admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for 24 h for observation and supportive care. Both infants demonstrated normal feeding, breathing, and bowel movements, with no signs of infection or bleeding. The twins were discharged in a good condition on the third day after birth.
Amniotic tissue specimens were sent for histopathological and cytogenetic analysis. Pathological examination confirmed that the tissue was composed of an amniotic membrane without chorionic elements. Examination of the placenta revealed no abnormalities, and cytogenetic analysis revealed a normal karyotype in both the mother and the twins. During the nine-month follow-up, the twins met their developmental milestones without any complications.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
This case report presents a rare and unusual case of fetal ingestion of an amniotic band in a monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) twin pregnancy. Typically, Amniotic Band Syndrome (ABS) manifests as fibrous bands that constrict and entrap fetal parts, leading to deformities, such as limb amputations [1, 2]. However, in this case, the unique event of a fetus swallowing an amniotic band deviated from the typical presentation of ABS, underscoring the complexity of managing such pregnancies.
The rarity of fetal ingestion of amniotic bands is evident, with only a few cases documented in the literature. Previous reports have highlighted the challenges in prenatally diagnosing such anomalies. For instance, Torpin et al. described a case in which a fibrous strand protruded from the newborn’s mouth only at delivery without prior prenatal detection [3]. Similarly, Boulet et al. reported the prenatal identification of a swallowed amniotic band as a cystic mass, which was later found wrapped around the neck at delivery [10]. These cases emphasize the diverse and atypical presentation of amniotic bands, stressing the need for vigilant prenatal monitoring.
The pathogenesis of ABS in MCDA twin pregnancies is notably complex, particularly in the absence of an evident rupture of the dividing membrane. Spontaneous septostomy, involving the rupture of the intertwin membrane, is a well-documented pathway to ABS, as the disrupted membrane can entangle fetal parts, leading to constrictive bands with severe outcomes, including deformities, amputations, or even fetal demise [8, 11–13]. Nakashima et al. (2021) elaborated on this mechanism by presenting a case where ABS developed in an MCDA twin pregnancy following an early second-trimester rupture of the dividing membrane. This case emphasizes how such a rupture could initiate the formation of amniotic bands that subsequently compromise one or both fetuses, supporting the extrinsic theory of ABS pathogenesis within the unique physiological context of MCDA twin pregnancies [14].
Moreover, iatrogenic factors must be considered in the development of ABS, particularly following invasive interventions such as fetoscopic laser surgery or septostomy used in managing TTTS [8, 15]. These procedures can lead to pseudoamniotic band syndrome (PABS), where disrupted amniotic membranes generate fibrous bands capable of constricting fetal limbs or the umbilical cord, complicating the pregnancy and increasing morbidity risks [16–18].
In the present case, the absence of any documented membrane rupture or invasive procedures related to the formation of amniotic bands suggested the possibility of alternative mechanisms. The literature suggests that amniotic bands may arise from rupture of the amniotic membrane, leading to fibrous strands that could either entangle fetal parts or be ingested by the fetus [3]. Additionally, Schüttauf and Hellebrekers (2015) documented a term infant who delivered with an amniotic band protruding from its mouth [19]. In their study, the separation of the chorioamniotic membrane was proposed as a potential cause, resulting in free-floating tissue bands that pose various fetal complications, such as the swallowing of an amniotic band.
We hypothesized that subtle weaknesses or microruptures in the membrane, potentially induced by mechanical stress, biochemical factors, or undetected uterine contractions, could have contributed to the formation of the amniotic bands in this case. These weaknesses or microruptures might have occurred earlier in pregnancy but were undetectable with current imaging techniques. This perspective aligns with the extrinsic model of ABS and underscores the need for further research to explore these potential mechanisms and their implications for fetal outcomes in complex pregnancies like those involving MCDA twins [3].
The critical importance of differential diagnosis in prenatal care is highlighted in this case, particularly in distinguishing between ABS, ingestion of an amniotic band, and other conditions that may present similarly on ultrasound [20, 21]. Conditions such as uterine adhesions, septate uterus, circumvallate placenta, and abnormal ultrasound appearance of the amniotic membranes must be carefully ruled out [22]. Advanced imaging techniques, including three-dimensional and four-dimensional ultrasonography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), provide enhanced visualization and complementary information, which are crucial for the accurate diagnosis of complex cases [14, 23]. These techniques can help identify subtle abnormalities or conditions that may not be easily detectable with standard ultrasound, improving the accuracy of diagnosis and effectiveness of management strategies [24–26].
In the management of cases involving the ingestion of amniotic bands, a set of unique challenges emerges, distinct from those typically associated with ABS. In our case, despite the absence of evident signs of fetal distress, a comprehensive assessment was conducted to evaluate the potential risks associated with prolonged amniotic band ingestion, including airway obstruction and gastrointestinal complications. These risks were carefully weighed against the benefits of continued uterine development. Considering the near-term gestational age (35 weeks and 5 days) and the unpredictable nature of this rare condition, a cautious approach favoring immediate intervention was adopted. As a result, an emergency cesarean section was performed, prioritizing fetal safety and aiming to minimize the potential complications associated with expectant management. Following the procedure, both twins successfully delivered and demonstrated satisfactory Apgar scores. Post-delivery, a 9-cm fibrous strand was promptly extracted from Twin A’s oral cavity, confirming the prenatal diagnosis and validating the interventional approach. This cautious approach underscores the importance of individualized care in managing complex pregnancies.
In conclusion, this case contributes to the limited literature on the fetal ingestion of amniotic bands in MCDA twin pregnancies. This highlights the importance of vigilant prenatal monitoring, accurate differential diagnosis, and individualized management strategies. The rarity and complexity of this condition necessitate further research to better understand its underlying pathophysiology and develop effective management protocols for optimizing maternal and fetal outcomes in similar cases.
