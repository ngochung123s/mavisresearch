TITLE: Outcomes of antenatal hydronephrosis and other renal abnormalities in a population‐based cohort

=== ABSTRACT ===
Hydronephrosis, commonly detected during second trimester ultrasound screenings, can signal underlying renal abnormalities. In 2018, Sweden introduced a national guideline recommending follow‐up for fetal renal pelves with an anteroposterior diameter (APD) ≥6 mm at the second trimester ultrasound. This study evaluates the impact of the guideline by assessing the incidence and resolution of hydronephrosis and other renal abnormalities. Secondary outcomes included postnatal renal function, frequency of febrile urinary tract infections (UTI), and the need for surgical intervention before the age of 2.
This prospective cohort study included fetuses diagnosed with hydronephrosis or other renal abnormalities during second trimester ultrasounds between November 2019 and October 2022. Maternal and child characteristics were compared with data from the Swedish Pregnancy Register. Pre‐ and postnatal outcomes up to 2 years of age were assessed for fetuses detected antenatally. Outcomes were compared to children diagnosed postnatally within the first 2 years of life and not identified prenatally.
Over 3 years, 31 094 second trimester ultrasounds were performed. Hydronephrosis or other renal anomalies were identified in 0.5% (n = 155); approximately 40% resolved by week 32, and seven additional third‐trimester detections brought the total number of antenatal cases to 162. By age 2, only 42% had persistent renal abnormalities. A retrospective ICD‐10 review identified 45 additional children diagnosed postnatally following symptoms before age 2, yielding an antenatal detection rate of 78.3% (162/207). Baseline demographics were similar between antenatal and postnatal groups and matched those in the Swedish Pregnancy Register. Antenatally detected cases showed diverse anomalies—primarily multicystic dysplastic kidney, duplex kidney, pelviureteric junction obstruction, and vesicoureteral reflux (VUR)—whereas postnatally detected cases were predominantly VUR. Febrile UTI requiring admission occurred in 11.8% of antenatal versus 71.7% of postnatal children (p < 0.0001); surgery rates were similar between groups.
The centralized structure of care enabled a comprehensive, population‐based cohort with near‐complete follow‐up. The detection rate of hydronephrosis during second trimester ultrasound is high, reaching ~80%. Given the high rate of spontaneous resolution, the national guideline threshold (APD ≥6 mm) appears to strike a reasonable balance between sensitivity and clinical practicality.

Second‐trimester ultrasound screening using APD ≥6 mm achieves high detection, high spontaneous resolution, and markedly fewer febrile UTIs, supporting a balanced and clinically practical guideline.


## INTRODUCTION
Hydronephrosis is one of the most frequently detected antenatal abnormalities, with previous studies reporting a prevalence of approximately 0.6–2.3% of fetuses during second trimester ultrasound screening. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The challenge lies in identifying clinically relevant cases, as the majority undergo spontaneous normalization during pregnancy. 2 , 6 , 7 , 8 However, monitoring antenatal hydronephrosis is crucial as it may signal more severe underlying renal abnormalities. 1 , 9 The majority (60–85%) of congenital renal and urinary tract abnormalities are detected antenatally through ultrasound. 10 , 11 , 12 Children with antenatally diagnosed hydronephrosis often remain asymptomatic, partly due to the early initiation of postnatal prophylactic antibiotics to prevent urinary tract infections (UTIs) and frequent follow‐up to monitor renal function. 1 , 3 In contrast, postnatally detected cases are typically identified because of symptoms, such as urinary tract infections, abdominal distension, or vomiting, while asymptomatic cases often remain undiagnosed. 13 , 14 Long‐term outcomes have been reported in the literature, with follow‐up extending up to 15 years, although data remain limited. 8 , 15
Guidelines for managing antenatal hydronephrosis vary significantly across regions and globally. The most prevalent antenatal diagnostic approach is measuring the anteroposterior diameter (APD) of the renal pelvis; however, the specific APD threshold for diagnosing hydronephrosis differs among guidelines. 16 In 2018, Sweden introduced a new guideline defining hydronephrosis as an APD ≥6 mm measured at the second trimester ultrasound in gestational age (GA) 18–20, with follow‐up ultrasound recommended at GA 32 (see Figure 1). This threshold is stricter than the previous Swedish threshold of APD ≥7 mm in GA 18–20, but lenient compared to international standards. In 2014, the United States revised its criteria, defining hydronephrosis as an APD ≥4 mm in the second trimester. 16 , 17 Similarly, the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) adopted the same cutoffs, recommending follow‐up for cases with an APD of ≥4 mm in the second trimester. 18

This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy and effectiveness of the current Swedish guideline for antenatal hydronephrosis and other renal abnormalities. It specifically assessed the incidence, rate of spontaneous resolution, postnatal renal function, occurrence of febrile UTIs, and the need for surgical intervention—while also accounting for cases that were not detected antenatally but diagnosed within the first 2 years of life.

## MATERIAL AND METHODS
This observational study was conducted at the Department of Women's Health at Sahlgrenska University Hospital and the affiliated Children's Hospital—the only facilities of their kind in the Gothenburg region, serving the entire western part of Sweden. All second trimester ultrasounds, showing hydronephrosis (APD ≥6 mm) or other renal abnormalities, between November 1, 2019, and October 31, 2022, were prospectively collected and included in the study, with the children being followed until the age of 2. Children diagnosed antenatally at a later GA were also included. Children from the same catchment area with a postnatally detected renal diagnosis due to symptoms identified before the age of 2 were retrospectively included using corresponding ICD‐10 codes between May 1, 2019, and June 1, 2024, to capture those not detected antenatally by ultrasound. Four terminations were performed after GA 18–20 due to detected abnormalities and were therefore not included in the study. To compare baseline demographics, data from the Swedish Pregnancy Register were utilized—encompassing all patients who delivered in Gothenburg during 2021; stillbirths and twins excluded. Each woman was represented only once, with her first delivery of the year being included.
The second trimester ultrasound was conducted between GA 18–20. Both this and the follow‐up ultrasound at GA 32 were performed by specially trained ultrasound midwives. In cases requiring an ultrasound by an obstetrician (see Figure 1), the procedure was carried out by a specifically trained obstetrician/fetal medicine specialist. The ultrasound equipment used included GE Voluson E6 and E10, and Philips EPIQ Elite. The number of ultrasound examinations conducted was registered only if the kidneys and urinary tracts were specifically examined. Children with persistent antenatal hydronephrosis or renal abnormalities, as well as those diagnosed postnatally, were followed according to a structured protocol. Follow‐up care was provided by specialists in pediatric surgery and pediatricians. Follow‐up assessments included repeated ultrasounds, voiding cystourethrography, and renal scintigraphy when clinically indicated. Postnatal renal function was routinely monitored by measuring serum creatinine concentrations. A retrospective review of the GA 18–20 ultrasound images was conducted for all the children who were detected postnatally to evaluate if hydronephrosis had been missed during primary assessment. This review was carried out by a specially trained ultrasound midwife.
Patients were included in the study if their GA 18–20 ultrasound was performed between November 1, 2019, and October 31, 2022, at the Department of Women's Health at Sahlgrenska University Hospital, and they subsequently delivered at the same hospital. Inclusion required detection of a renal abnormality either during the GA 18–20 ultrasound, at a later antenatal ultrasound performed for other reasons, or detection postnatally before age two. Secondary referrals to the maternity unit or Children's University Hospital during the study period were excluded.
Hydronephrosis was defined as a renal APD ≥6 mm at GA 18–20. Because it can be challenging to distinguish hydronephrosis from other upper or lower urinary tract or renal malformations at this early stage, all such conditions—even when the APD was <6 mm—were included. If warning signs were observed (see Figure 1) or if the renal APD ≥10 mm, an immediate follow‐up was scheduled with a specifically trained obstetrician/fetal medicine specialist, either on the same day or the next day after the initial ultrasound. The obstetric team then coordinated with pediatric specialists to determine appropriate intervals for further evaluations. If the dilation was <10 mm and no warning signs were present, an additional ultrasound follow‐up was scheduled at GA 32. Hydronephrosis at GA 32 and at the postnatal ultrasound was defined as an APD ≥10 mm. Children with missing postnatal follow‐up were conservatively classified as having persistent abnormalities. This approach was chosen to avoid underestimating the prevalence of persistent renal abnormalities.
The study collected data on various maternal and child characteristics. Maternal variables included parity, age, body mass index, in vitro fertilization, smoking in early pregnancy, mode of delivery, and gestational age at birth. Child‐related factors, such as sex, birthweight, and small‐for‐gestational‐age according to Marsal et al., 19 were also recorded. APD of the renal pelvis was documented at the following time points; conducted approximately at GA 18–20, GA 32, 4 weeks of life, and 2 years of age. Additionally, information regarding other renal abnormalities was also collected at these time points. Data regarding chromosomal deviations were obtained both antenatally and postnatally. The final postnatal diagnosis was also documented. Clinical outcomes were evaluated, including the requirement for inpatient care due to febrile UTI, defined as the presence of fever (>38°C) and a urine dipstick and a confirmed positive urine culture. 20 Additional clinical outcomes included the use of antibiotic prophylaxis, impaired renal function at the age of two (defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2, calculated using the Schwartz formula 21 ), and surgical interventions performed before the age of two related to the renal abnormality.
The continuous variables were described by mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and range, while categorical variables were described by counts and percentages. For tests between two groups, Fisher's exact test was used for binary variables, Chi‐squared for categorical variables, t‐test for normally distributed continuous variables, and Mann–Whitney U‐test for skewed continuous variables. All tests were two‐tailed and conducted at a significance level of 0.05. Analyses were conducted on available data only; missing values were not imputed. All analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). APNC Sweden AB provided support in the statistical analyses.

## RESULTS
A total of 31 094 GA 18–20 ultrasound examinations were conducted during the study period. Hydronephrosis or other renal abnormalities were detected in 155 fetuses during GA 18–20 (0.5%). Seven additional cases were identified later in pregnancy—during the third trimester, when ultrasound was performed for other indications—resulting in a total antenatal incidence of 0.5% (162/31 094).
Of the 155 fetuses detected at GA 18–20, 61 (39.4%) showed resolution by GA 32 (see Figure 2). Four children were born before GA 32 and were therefore followed immediately after delivery and conservatively classified as having persistent abnormalities until postnatal follow‐up. Among the remaining cases detectable at GA 32, further normalization occurred after birth (see Figure 2). By 2 years of age, 58.1% (90/155) of children detected at GA 18–20 had shown complete resolution of hydronephrosis or other renal abnormalities. Of the seven cases detected during the third trimester, four showed no persisting abnormality at 2 years of age. Overall, 58.0% (94/162) of all antenatally detected cases normalized within the first 2 years of life.

Among the 155 fetuses identified at the GA 18–20 ultrasound, hydronephrosis was present in 118 (76.1%), corresponding to an overall incidence of 0.38% (118/31 094). Other renal abnormalities than hydronephrosis were present in 37/155, 23.8%. Of the 155 children with hydronephrosis and other renal abnormalities detected at the GA 18–20 ultrasound, 89 had unilateral hydronephrosis. Among these, 44/89 (49.4%) showed spontaneous resolution by GA 32, and an additional 15 resolved postnatally—resulting in 30 children (30/89, 33.7%) with persistent abnormalities at 2 years of age. Bilateral hydronephrosis was observed in 29 children; of these, 12 (12/29, 41.4%) normalized by GA 32, and an additional nine resolved postnatally, resulting in 27.6% (8/29) with persisting abnormalities at age of 2.
In addition to the antenatal cohort, 45 children were diagnosed postnatally during the first 2 years of life, resulting in a total of 207 children (see Figure 3) meeting the inclusion criteria. Of these, 162 were detected antenatally and 45 postnatally, giving an antenatal detection rate of 78.3% (162/207).
Among the 162 children with antenatal hydronephrosis and other renal abnormalities detected antenatally, 135 were identified through prospective data collection, while 27 were identified retrospectively through corresponding ICD codes. These 27 children had all been diagnosed antenatally and followed according to the guideline but were not captured in the prospective dataset. Their retrospective identification confirms that each of them had a persistent renal abnormality verified postnatally.
Out of the 207 children included in the study, 34 had not yet reached age two at data collection. Of these 34 cases, data collection was performed when the youngest child was 1.5 years old, with a median age of 1.7 years. Additionally, three children were only followed until delivery and had no postnatal follow‐up, and two missed their scheduled two‐year follow‐up. However, all data collected up to the point of dropout were included in the analysis. Children with missing postnatal follow‐up were conservatively classified as having persistent abnormalities. Of the 45 children with postnatally detected abnormalities, a review of ultrasound images from the GA 18–20 ultrasound revealed hydronephrosis and/or other renal findings in only one additional case 2.2% (1/45).
Baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between the study groups or in comparison with the national pregnancy registry (see Table 1). Maternal age, smoking in early pregnancy, preterm birth (gestational age < 37 + 0), and small‐for‐gestational‐age rates were slightly higher than in the Swedish Pregnancy Register, but these differences were not statistically significant. No significant differences were found between the antenatally and postnatally detected cases either. Overall, 62.8% of the children were male.

Among the 162 children detected antenatally, three (3/162, 1.9%) had a chromosomal abnormality, all identified antenatally. In the postnatal group, four children were diagnosed with chromosomal abnormalities (4/45, 8.9%), all postnatally identified. This resulted in a total cohort incidence of 7/207 (3.4%). Two of the antenatally detected cases showed resolution of the renal abnormality during pregnancy, while the third had persistent findings, a multicystic dysplastic kidney (MCDK).
Among 148 children with persistent renal abnormalities, 102 were antenatally detected with persistent abnormality at birth. The most common postnatal diagnosis was vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), found in 44 of 148 children (29.7%), with a marked predominance in the postnatal group (32/46, 69.6%) compared with the antenatal group (12/102, 11.8%). MCDK was the second most common, diagnosed in 25 children (25/148, 16.9%), with only one in the postnatal group. Duplex kidney (with or without ureterocele) was diagnosed in 17 children (17/148, 11.5%)—13 in the antenatal group and 4 in the postnatal group. Pelviureteric junction obstruction (PUJO) was found in 13 children (8.8%), one in the postnatal group. A wider range of less common renal anomalies was also identified, including ectopic and dysplastic kidneys, horseshoe kidney, ureterocele, and single kidney. Children with antenatal findings that normalized during the first 2 years of life did not receive a postnatal diagnosis.

All measurements were compared with the baseline APD at 18–20 weeks. The analysis shows that the Least Squares (LS) mean differences for VUR tend to increase over time, with the largest difference observed near 2 years of age (p = 0.2), although not statistically significant at any time point. In terms of antibiotic prophylaxis, 52% (53/102) of children in the antenatal group started prophylaxis neonatally before the first week of life. In contrast, no child in the postnatal group received prophylaxis during this period. In the antenatal group, an additional 3.9% (4/102) of children commenced prophylaxis after the first week of life, all in response to infection. In contrast, 67.4% (31/46) of children in the postnatal group were initiated on prophylaxis after the first week, following either a febrile UTI or postnatal detection of hydronephrosis or another renal abnormality. Among the 102 children that were antenatally detected with persistent hydronephrosis or other renal abnormalities at birth, 11.8% (n = 12) required inpatient care for UTIs (see Table 3). In the postnatal group, 71.7% (n = 33) of the 46 children required inpatient care for febrile UTIs. There was no difference in the need for surgery before the age of two, with 9.8% (n = 10) in the antenatal group requiring surgery, compared to 13.0% (n = 6) in the postnatal group. Reduced renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2) was identified in three children. Two (2%, 2/102) of these were detected antenatally, while one (2.2%, 1/46) was detected postnatally.

## DISCUSSION
The study found a lower‐than‐previously‐reported prevalence of antenatal hydronephrosis and other renal abnormalities at GA 18–20. A high rate of normalization was observed, particularly by GA 32 where it was seen in almost 40%, and by the age of 2 more than half of the children showed complete resolution. Unilateral and bilateral hydronephrosis both showed high rates of spontaneous normalization, with only 33.7% and 27.6% respectively exhibiting persistent abnormalities at 2 years of age. The guideline showed high diagnostic accuracy, as nearly 80% of cases were detected antenatally. A higher detection rate may be challenging to achieve, as significant diagnostic variation was observed between groups: VUR was primarily diagnosed postnatally, whereas MCDK, duplex kidney and PUJO were more commonly identified antenatally. No significant baseline demographic differences were found between the antenatal and postnatal groups compared to the background population. However, children detected antenatally had fewer febrile UTIs, although the need for surgical intervention did not differ.
The incidence of antenatal hydronephrosis and other renal abnormalities at the GA 18–20 ultrasound was 0.5% while the incidence of hydronephrosis alone was 0.38%, lower than the 0.83–2.3% reported in earlier studies. 2 , 3 , 4 This lower incidence is consistent with the stricter diagnostic threshold (APD ≥6 mm) used in this national guideline and may at least partly be explained by this difference in cut‐off. In contrast, Arora et al., Green et al., and Sairam et al. all applied a 4 mm cut‐off, reporting incidence rates of 0.83%, 1.3%, and 2.3%, respectively. 2 , 4 , 8 These studies primarily focused on hydronephrosis alone, whereas our study included a broader range of renal abnormalities.
Around 40% of cases normalized between GA 18–20 and GA 32. Sairam et al. reported that 80% of cases with an APD of 4–6.9 mm resolved during pregnancy, with an additional 80% of the remainder resolving postnatally, while no antenatal resolution occurred in cases with APD ≥7 mm. 2 Green et al. reported 57% normalization at GA 32 and 17% persistence postnatally when using a lower APD threshold of ≥4 mm at the second trimester and ≥7 mm at third trimester. 8 Although Green et al., Sairam et al., and the present study apply different APD thresholds for defining hydronephrosis, higher normalization rates in studies with lower cutoffs suggest that such thresholds may increase prenatal detection without improving identification of children needing postnatal care.
Two cases (3.3%) were false negatives, with abnormalities re‐diagnosed postnatally despite apparent resolution by GA 32, suggesting limited sensitivity of the current Swedish guideline. Similarly, Green et al. reported a 1.5% false‐negative rate using lower APD thresholds. 8 Therefore, lowering the APD threshold may not fully eliminate the risk of false negatives.
Our study showed that VUR was most often diagnosed postnatally, while MCDK duplex kidney and PUJO were more frequently detected antenatally. This aligns with earlier findings linking VUR to increased UTI risk, 22 also reflected in our results (11.8% in the antenatal group vs. 71.7% in the postnatal group). VUR is challenging to detect antenatally due to the limited sensitivity of ultrasound examinations and bladder‐related variability in renal APD. 23 This was supported by our retrospective review, where only 1 of 45 postnatally diagnosed cases showed an abnormality on the GA 18–20 ultrasound. Similarly, a prior study found that only approximately 25% of high‐grade VUR cases were diagnosed antenatally despite frequent renal dysplasia. 24
A total of 409 ultrasound assessments, in addition to the GA 18–20 ultrasounds, were conducted over a three‐year period. While this initially may raise concerns regarding resource use and the potential for unnecessary parental anxiety, 25 it must be viewed in the context of the total number of GA 18–20 ultrasounds performed during the same period—31 094 in total. This indicates that the increased surveillance was manageable and appears reasonable and clinically justified. This is consistent with findings by Ravi de Roo et al., who demonstrated that a 7 mm cut‐off can be considered clinically safe and appropriate for follow‐up. 26
A limitation is that 27 children identified with hydronephrosis at the GA 18–20 ultrasound were initially missed in the prospective data collection and later included through ICD‐10 codes. This may have led to underreporting of spontaneous resolution by GA 32 and a slight underestimation of the true incidence, which is unlikely to exceed 1–2% and may only marginally exceed the observed 0.5%. Additionally, not all children were 2 years of age at follow‐up, meaning some cases of febrile UTIs or surgery might have been missed. However, the study had a relatively long follow‐up, with the youngest child being 1.5 years old at data collection—considerably longer than in the study by Green et al., which followed participants up to 6 months of age. 8 The need for surgical intervention may increase in long‐term follow‐up, though most cases needing surgery are identified early in life. 27
A major strength of this study is the epidemiological population‐based design, capturing all cases from a single maternity unit and pediatric hospital. The low rate of missing data and the inclusion of a socioeconomically and medically diverse population further strengthen its validity. This study is the first to evaluate Sweden's current national guidelines and provides valuable clinical insight into antenatal detection and outcomes of renal abnormalities.

## CONCLUSION
Antenatally detected hydronephrosis and other renal abnormalities showed a favorable prognosis. The guideline appears clinically appropriate, achieving nearly 80% detection. Normalization before age two was common, with only approximately 40% showing persistent abnormalities. Most missed cases involved VUR, which is challenging to detect antenatally. Fewer UTIs in the antenatal group likely reflect diagnostic differences between groups. Implementation led to only 409 additional follow‐ups—just a fraction of the 31 094 second trimester ultrasounds conducted over the three‐year period. Any further lowering of the APD threshold should be carefully balanced against the risk of increased follow‐up for cases with limited long‐term clinical relevance.
